# Persona 02: David Whelan — Experienced Investor

## Demographics

- **Age:** 52
- **Location:** Jacksonville, FL (owns a home in a gated community in Ponte Vedra Beach)
- **Occupation:** Full-time land investor (sole proprietor, LLC structured)
- **Income:** ~$280K/year from land flips and seller-financed notes
- **Device:** Desktop PC (Windows 11, dual 27" monitors), iPhone 13 (rarely used for business)
- **Browser:** Chrome with 23 tabs open at any given time

## Background

David spent 20 years in commercial insurance before burning out at 44. He'd been buying land on the side since 2016 — started with a $3,500 parcel in Polk County after listening to a podcast. By 2018, he was doing two deals a month. By 2020, he quit his insurance job. He's now a full-time land investor who closes 10-20 deals per year with an average profit margin of 180-300%.

He has a system. It's not elegant, but it works:
- A 47-tab Google Sheets workbook that tracks his entire pipeline from mailer → lead → due diligence → offer → closing → disposition
- County assessor websites bookmarked for 14 Florida counties plus 6 counties in Georgia and Alabama
- A VA in the Philippines (Reina) who pulls tax records, checks for liens, and does initial comps research. He pays her $800/month.
- PropStream for comps (he pays $99/month and considers it overpriced)
- A local title company he's used for 6 years

David understands land at a level most software developers never will. He knows that a 5-acre parcel in Levy County with "agricultural" zoning and no road frontage is worth roughly $3,500-5,000, and he knows why. He can spot a bad comp in 2 seconds — "that one sold to an adjacent landowner, it's not a market comp." He knows which counties have fast closings and which ones take 90 days because they have one clerk who processes everything.

## How He Found AcreOS

David is in a Discord group of ~40 experienced land investors. Someone shared a link to AcreOS and said "anybody tried this?" Two people said they'd signed up but hadn't used it seriously. David bookmarked it. Two weeks later, Reina told him she was going to take a month off to visit family. David decided to evaluate whether AcreOS could handle some of Reina's research tasks while she was away.

He is not looking for a new system. He is evaluating whether AcreOS can slot into his existing workflow as a research tool. He will not restructure his process around AcreOS. AcreOS has to fit into his process, or he'll pass.

## Technical Comfort Level

**Moderate.** David is not a technologist, but he's been using PropStream, DataTree, county GIS portals, Google Earth, and spreadsheets for years. He knows how to navigate complex data interfaces. He is comfortable with maps, parcel data, legal descriptions, and property records. He does not know what an API is and he doesn't care. He's not interested in integrations or automations. He wants to see data on a screen, verify it, and make decisions.

He has a deep distrust of AI specifically. He watched a YouTube video where someone used ChatGPT to analyze a land deal and it hallucinated a road that didn't exist. He's also seen AI-generated comps that used residential lot sales as comparables for raw acreage. He now treats any AI output as "guilty until proven innocent."

## Expectations Shaped by Other Products

David's mental model comes from PropStream, county assessor websites, and Google Earth. He expects:

- **Raw data access.** He wants to see the actual assessed value, the actual tax amount, the actual sale history — not a summary or a score. If AcreOS shows a "Deal Score: 87/100," his first question is "based on what?" and his second question is "let me see the inputs."
- **Source attribution.** Every data point should tell him where it came from. "Assessed value: $12,400 (Levy County Property Appraiser, 2025)" — not just "$12,400."
- **Data recency.** He needs to know when the data was last updated. If AcreOS shows 2023 assessed values when the county has already published 2025 values, he'll consider the entire platform unreliable.
- **Comp transparency.** If AcreOS suggests comparable sales, he wants to see each comp individually — the APN, the sale date, the sale price, the acreage, the zoning, the distance from the subject parcel. He will check these against county records.
- **No hand-holding.** He doesn't want onboarding tours or tooltips. He wants to get to the data. If there's a 5-step wizard before he can search a parcel, he's annoyed.

## Goal for Using AcreOS

David has three specific use cases he wants to evaluate:

1. **Comp research acceleration.** Currently, Reina spends 2-3 hours per parcel pulling comps from county records and building a comp sheet. If AcreOS can do this in minutes with reliable, source-attributed comps, it would save him $300-400/month in VA time.

2. **Due diligence checklist automation.** He wants to know: Are there liens? Is there road access? What's the flood zone? What's the zoning? Are there back taxes? Currently he checks 8-12 sources per parcel. If AcreOS aggregates these, great — but only if the data is current and sourced.

3. **New market evaluation.** He's thinking about expanding into Texas and New Mexico. He doesn't know those markets. He wants to use AcreOS to understand county-level pricing patterns, days on market, and volume — the kind of macro research that would take him weeks to do manually.

He is explicitly NOT looking for:
- AI to tell him whether a deal is "good"
- Automated offer generation
- CRM features (he has his spreadsheet)
- Marketing tools

## Realistic Failure Modes

1. **The data mismatch.** David searches a parcel he already owns in Putnam County, FL. He knows the assessed value is $8,200 (he checked last month). AcreOS shows $7,400. He immediately questions every other number on the platform. He checks two more parcels he owns. If even one more number is off, he's done. He'll tell the Discord group "data's stale, don't bother."

2. **The black-box comp.** AcreOS shows "Estimated Market Value: $14,500" for a parcel. David clicks to see comps. The comps include a 0.25-acre residential lot that sold for $45,000 two miles away. He knows that's not a valid comp for a 5-acre rural parcel. He concludes the AI doesn't understand land — it's treating land like residential real estate.

3. **The unsourced claim.** AcreOS says "No liens found" for a parcel. David asks: where did you check? If the answer is "we checked [some third-party API]" rather than "we checked the Putnam County Clerk of Court records," he doesn't trust it. He's seen third-party lien searches miss local utility liens and special assessment districts.

4. **The slow workflow.** David evaluates 5-8 parcels per day. If the AcreOS workflow for pulling up a parcel, reviewing data, and exporting a summary takes more than 4-5 minutes per parcel, it's slower than his current process. He won't switch to something slower.

5. **The forced funnel.** AcreOS tries to push him into a workflow he doesn't want. "Add this parcel to your pipeline!" "Set up your deal criteria!" He doesn't want a pipeline in AcreOS. He has a spreadsheet. He wants AcreOS to be a research tool, not a project management tool.

## What Would Make Him Abandon

- **Inaccurate data.** One confirmed mismatch between AcreOS data and county records will put him on high alert. Two will make him leave. He will tell other investors.
- **AI confidence without evidence.** Any AI-generated claim without source attribution and a way to verify it independently. "Trust me" is the fastest way to lose David.
- **No raw data access.** If he can't see the underlying data behind a summary or score, he treats the summary as fiction.
- **Stale data.** If assessed values or tax records are more than one fiscal year behind what's on county websites, the platform is useless to him.
- **Wasted time.** If AcreOS adds steps to his workflow without saving steps elsewhere, it's a net negative.

## What Would Make Him Stay

- A parcel detail page where he can see assessed value, tax history, sale history, zoning, flood zone, and legal description — all with source attribution and last-updated timestamps.
- Comp results that he can filter by acreage range, distance, sale date, and property type — with the ability to exclude individual comps and see how the estimate changes.
- The ability to search by APN or legal description, not just address (many rural parcels don't have addresses).
- A due diligence report he can export as a PDF or CSV and hand to his title company.
- Data that matches what he sees on county assessor sites. Not close. Matches.

## Signature Quote

"Show me the source."

## Testing Priority

David tests **data accuracy, source attribution, comp quality, and workflow efficiency.** If AcreOS can earn David's trust on data quality, it has a shot at becoming part of his daily workflow. If it can't, he's the kind of user who will actively warn others away.
