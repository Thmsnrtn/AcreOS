# Persona 03: Jenna Okafor — Aspirational Investor

## Demographics

- **Age:** 29
- **Location:** Seattle, WA (Capitol Hill, renting a 1BR apartment)
- **Occupation:** Senior Product Designer at a mid-stage fintech startup (Series C, 300 employees)
- **Income:** $135K/year + RSUs
- **Savings:** $50K in a HYSA, $22K in a Roth IRA (target-date fund), no other investments
- **Device:** MacBook Pro 14" (personal), iPhone 14 (heavy use), iPad Air (weekend browsing)
- **Browser:** Chrome on all devices, synced tabs

## Background

Jenna grew up in suburban Maryland, got a BFA in Graphic Design from MICA, then pivoted to UX/product design. She's been in tech for 6 years and is good at her job — she designs financial products and thinks deeply about information hierarchy, user mental models, and progressive disclosure. This means she evaluates every product she touches through a professional lens. She notices inconsistent spacing, unclear affordances, and confusing navigation — not because she's picky, but because it's literally her job.

She's single, lives alone, and her biggest financial anxiety is that she's "behind." Her college friends who went into finance are buying condos. Her coworker who joined a pre-IPO startup just exercised options worth $400K. Jenna has a solid salary and good savings habits but no assets generating returns outside her retirement account. She's been consuming financial independence content (ChooseFI podcast, r/financialindependence, a few YouTube channels) and the idea of owning income-producing assets appeals to her deeply.

She's researched real estate broadly — considered rental properties (too expensive in Seattle, too much management), REITs (too passive, doesn't feel like "real" ownership), and house hacking (she doesn't want a roommate). A TikTok creator she follows mentioned land flipping as "the lowest barrier to entry in real estate" and she went down a rabbit hole. She watched 4 YouTube videos, read 2 blog posts, and decided to explore it seriously.

## How She Found AcreOS

Jenna Googled "land investing software" and AcreOS came up. She also found LandWatch, LandModo, and a few others. She opened all of them in tabs. She'll evaluate AcreOS the way she evaluates any product: land on it, form an impression in 10 seconds, explore for 5 minutes, decide whether to invest more time. She's not comparing features in a spreadsheet — she's comparing feelings. Which one feels like a product built by people who care?

## Technical Comfort Level

**Very high for digital products, beginner for real estate.** Jenna can navigate any SaaS product intuitively. She understands design patterns, data visualization, and information architecture at a professional level. She can tell when a dashboard is well-organized and when it's a dumping ground.

But she has zero real estate knowledge. She doesn't know:
- What an APN is
- How county assessor websites work
- What "due diligence" means in a land context (she associates it vaguely with corporate M&A)
- How land is actually purchased (Does she need a realtor? A title company? Can she do it online?)
- What "seller financing" means or why it matters
- How to evaluate whether a parcel is a good deal

She will Google every unfamiliar term. She'll open a new tab, search "what is an APN in real estate," read the top result, and come back. If she has to do this more than 3-4 times in a single session, she'll start feeling like the product expects knowledge she doesn't have, and she'll categorize it as "not for beginners."

## Expectations Shaped by Other Products

Jenna's reference products are Robinhood, Wealthfront, Notion, and Figma. She expects:

- **Opinionated onboarding.** Don't show her a blank canvas. Ask her a few questions (budget, location interest, experience level) and then configure the experience for her. Robinhood asks what you want to invest in. Wealthfront asks your risk tolerance. AcreOS should ask something.
- **Education embedded in the product.** Not a separate "Knowledge Base" link — actual contextual education. When she sees "Flood Zone: X," she wants a small info icon that explains what Flood Zone X means and whether it's good or bad for her.
- **Visual quality as trust signal.** Jenna consciously uses UI quality as a heuristic for product quality. A well-designed product signals "this team is competent and cares about details." A poorly designed product signals "this might be a side project or a scam." She will literally tell friends "the app looks legit" or "it feels sketchy" based on visual impression alone.
- **Responsive across devices.** She'll start researching on her MacBook, save something, then check it on her phone during her commute, then look at it again on her iPad on the couch. The experience should be seamless across all three.
- **Progress and momentum.** She wants to feel like she's building toward something. A progress bar, a checklist, a "you've completed 3 of 7 steps" — something that gives her a sense of forward motion.

## Goal for Using AcreOS

Jenna wants to go from "interested in land investing" to "made my first purchase" within 2-3 months. She has $50K available but would probably start with a parcel in the $3K-8K range to learn the process with low stakes.

Her ideal experience:
1. **Learn:** Understand the basics of land investing through the product itself
2. **Explore:** Browse available opportunities or search for land that fits her criteria
3. **Evaluate:** Use AcreOS tools to determine if a specific parcel is a good deal
4. **Act:** Get clear guidance on how to actually purchase the parcel (or at least know the next steps)

She's not in a rush, but she doesn't want to feel stuck. If she opens AcreOS on a Saturday morning with 2 hours to spend, she wants to make meaningful progress in those 2 hours.

## Realistic Failure Modes

1. **The expertise assumption.** Jenna signs up and the dashboard assumes she has parcels, has a pipeline, has criteria defined. Everything is structured around an existing workflow she doesn't have. She feels like she walked into the middle of a movie and has no idea what's happening.

2. **The jargon spiral.** She encounters a screen with terms like "assessed value," "market value," "tax deed," "quiet title," and "easement" — all on the same page, none explained. She Googles one, which leads to another unfamiliar term, which leads to another. After 10 minutes she's learned some vocabulary but hasn't made any progress in the app. She closes it feeling educated but frustrated.

3. **The confidence gap.** AcreOS shows her a parcel and says "Estimated Value: $6,200." She has no framework to evaluate whether that's right. She doesn't know if $6,200 for 2 acres in rural Oregon is a good deal or a terrible one. The app shows her the number but doesn't help her understand it. She's paralyzed by not knowing what she doesn't know.

4. **The missing bridge to action.** She finds a parcel she likes. Now what? Does she call someone? Does she make an offer through the app? Does she need a lawyer? AcreOS shows her data but doesn't show her the path from "I like this parcel" to "I own this parcel." She's stuck at the gap between research and action.

5. **The weekend warrior penalty.** She uses AcreOS on Saturday, makes progress, then doesn't open it for 9 days. When she comes back, she can't remember where she was or what she was doing. There's no "welcome back, here's where you left off" state. She has to reconstruct her context from scratch, which feels like starting over.

## What Would Make Her Abandon

- **Feeling stupid.** If the product makes her feel like she doesn't belong or doesn't know enough to use it, she'll leave. She won't blame herself — she'll blame the product for not meeting her where she is.
- **No learning path.** If AcreOS is purely a tool (search, analyze, manage) with no educational scaffolding, she'll decide she needs to "learn more first" and go watch YouTube videos instead. She may never come back.
- **Visual red flags.** Broken layouts, inconsistent typography, low-quality maps, generic stock imagery. Any of these will trigger her "this feels sketchy" response.
- **No clear first step.** If after signing up she doesn't know exactly what to do first, she'll bounce. She needs a nudge, not a blank canvas.
- **Isolation.** If she feels like she's doing this completely alone with no community, no support, and no indication that other beginners have succeeded, the risk feels too high.

## What Would Make Her Stay

- An onboarding flow that asks "Have you invested in land before?" and adjusts the experience based on her answer.
- A "Your First Deal" guided pathway that walks her through the process: learn the basics, define your criteria, find a parcel, evaluate it, make an offer, close the deal.
- Contextual glossary — not a separate page, but inline definitions that appear when she hovers or taps on terms she might not know.
- Beautiful, thoughtful design that communicates competence. Clean typography, good spacing, purposeful color, data visualizations that respect the data.
- A sample deal she can walk through — "Here's a real deal that a user completed. Here's what they paid, what they sold for, and the timeline" — so she can build a mental model before risking her own money.
- A "Save for later" or "Watchlist" feature so she can bookmark parcels and come back to them on her next session without losing context.

## Signature Quote

"The app looks legit."

## Testing Priority

Jenna tests **beginner onboarding, educational scaffolding, cross-device experience, and the gap between research and action.** She represents the largest potential growth segment for AcreOS: tech-savvy young professionals with capital and motivation but no real estate experience. If the product can guide her from curiosity to first deal, it can guide thousands like her.
