# Persona 04: Robert "Bobby" Tate — Retired Buy-and-Hold

## Demographics

- **Age:** 68
- **Location:** Crossville, TN (population 12,000; Cumberland County)
- **Occupation:** Retired school principal (35 years in public education, retired 2021)
- **Income:** $54K/year (state pension + Social Security), plus ~$6K/year in lease income from two parcels
- **Savings:** $180K in a traditional IRA, $40K in savings
- **Device:** iPad Air (5th gen, 2022) with a keyboard case, set to largest text size. Does not own a laptop or desktop computer. Has an iPhone SE (3rd gen) but primarily uses it for calls and texts.
- **Browser:** Safari on iPad. Does not use tabs. Opens one thing at a time.

## Background

Robert was born and raised in Cumberland County. He taught high school social studies for 12 years, then became an assistant principal, then a principal — first at a middle school, then at Crossville's main high school, where he served for 18 years before retiring. He's deeply respected in the community. People call him "Mr. Tate" or "Bobby" depending on whether they were his students or his peers.

He started buying land in the early 1990s. His father-in-law, a farmer, told him "they're not making any more of it" and sold him 8 acres at a below-market price. Robert held it. Over the next decade, he bought more — always in Cumberland County or neighboring counties (Fentress, White, Putnam). He'd hear about a parcel at church or at the hardware store, drive out to look at it, and make an offer if the price was right. He never flipped. He bought and held.

Today he owns 12 parcels totaling about 95 acres. Two parcels are leased to a local farmer for hay. The rest sit — some have timber value, some are just land. He pays property taxes on all of them (total ~$2,800/year across all parcels). His records are:

- A manila folder for each parcel containing the deed, the most recent tax bill, and sometimes a hand-drawn map
- A spiral notebook where he logs tax payments
- Phone numbers for the county tax assessor's office and the register of deeds, whom he calls when he needs information

He has never used a spreadsheet. He has never visited a county assessor website. When he needs information about one of his parcels, he calls the county office during business hours and asks a person.

## How He Found AcreOS

Robert's son, Marcus (39, IT project manager in Nashville), has been telling him for years that he needs to "get organized" with his properties. Marcus is concerned that if something happened to Robert, nobody would know what he owns or what it's worth. At Christmas 2025, Marcus sat down with Robert, pulled up AcreOS on Robert's iPad, and set up an account for him. Marcus entered two of Robert's parcels before he had to leave. He told Robert, "Just poke around, Dad. It's pretty easy."

Robert has opened AcreOS twice since then. The first time, he stared at the dashboard for 3 minutes, didn't know what to click, and closed it. The second time, he tapped on one of the parcels Marcus entered and saw some information — assessed value, acreage, a map — and thought "well, that's neat." He hasn't been back in two weeks.

## Technical Comfort Level

**Low.** Robert is not incompetent — he ran a school with 1,200 students and 80 staff for 18 years. He's organized, methodical, and patient. But his organizing systems are physical, not digital. He uses his iPad for:

- Email (checks it once a day, writes emails with one finger, uses full sentences and proper punctuation)
- Weather app
- Facebook (reads but rarely posts; looks at photos of grandchildren)
- Safari to look up things occasionally ("what time does Lowe's close")
- FaceTime with Marcus and his daughter in Knoxville

He does not:
- Use any productivity software
- Understand what a "dashboard" is in a software context
- Know what "AI" means in practice (he associates it with ChatGPT, which he's seen negative news coverage about — "that thing that makes stuff up")
- Use cloud storage
- Understand why things need passwords or why he can't just have one password for everything

His iPad is set to the largest font size. He wears reading glasses. He reads every word on the screen — he does not skim. He taps carefully and deliberately. When something unexpected happens (a popup, a modal, an animation he didn't expect), he stops and looks for a way to get back to where he was.

## Expectations Shaped by Other Products

Robert's reference points are not other software products — they're other service relationships:

- **His bank branch.** He walks in, tells the teller what he needs, and they help him. If he's confused, they explain it in plain language. They know his name.
- **The county assessor's office.** He calls, gives them a parcel number or his name, and they tell him what he needs to know. They're patient.
- **His insurance agent.** He meets in person once a year. The agent explains changes in plain English, shows him the relevant numbers, and handles the paperwork.

From software specifically, he expects:
- **Plain language.** Not "pipeline," not "due diligence module," not "AI-powered insights." Tell him in words he'd use at the kitchen table: "Your properties," "What they're worth," "Taxes you owe."
- **Nothing hidden.** He doesn't understand nested menus, hamburger icons, or progressive disclosure. If something exists, he wants to see it. He doesn't know to look for a three-dot menu.
- **No surprises.** He doesn't want things to move, animate, or change unless he explicitly did something. Auto-refreshing data or real-time updates would confuse him.
- **A phone number.** If he gets stuck, he wants to call someone. Not chat. Not email. Call.

## Goal for Using AcreOS

Robert's goal is simple and specific. He has said it out loud to Marcus:

**"I just want to see my properties and know what they're worth."**

Expanded, this means:
1. **A single place where all 12 of his parcels are listed** with the basic information: location, acreage, and what he paid for each one (if he remembers).
2. **Current estimated value for each parcel** so he can understand his total land wealth for estate planning purposes. His attorney has asked him for this.
3. **Tax payment tracking** so he can see when taxes are due and whether they've been paid, without calling the county.
4. **Something Marcus can access** if Robert is incapacitated. He wants his son to be able to log in and see everything.

He is explicitly NOT looking for:
- Buying or selling tools
- Market analysis
- AI recommendations
- Anything related to deals, flips, or investing strategy
- Mobile notifications

## Realistic Failure Modes

1. **The vocabulary barrier.** Robert opens AcreOS and sees navigation items like "Dashboard," "Pipeline," "Due Diligence," "Analytics." He doesn't know what any of these mean in this context. He's looking for something labeled "My Properties" or "My Land." If he can't find it in the first 30 seconds, he'll close the app and call Marcus.

2. **The small-target problem.** Robert taps the wrong thing because buttons, links, or icons are too small or too close together. On his iPad with large text, the layout might be cramped. He accidentally navigates somewhere he didn't intend, doesn't know how to get back, and feels frustrated and embarrassed.

3. **The AI trust barrier.** AcreOS shows a value estimate for one of his parcels. Robert knows the land — he's walked it dozens of times, he knows the neighbors, he knows what his neighbor sold a comparable piece for in 2019. If the AI estimate is significantly different from what he believes (either too high or too low), he won't think "maybe the AI is considering factors I'm not." He'll think "this thing doesn't know what it's talking about" and distrust the entire platform.

4. **The data entry wall.** Marcus entered 2 parcels but Robert has 12. To enter the remaining 10, he might need to provide APNs, addresses, or legal descriptions. He has the deeds in manila folders, but he doesn't know how to look at a deed and find the information AcreOS is asking for. The field labels on the form don't match the language on his deeds. He gives up after the first one.

5. **The accidental action.** Robert accidentally deletes a parcel, changes a setting, or triggers an action he didn't understand. There's no confirmation step, or the confirmation dialog uses language he doesn't parse quickly. He panics and calls Marcus, who may not answer for hours.

6. **The session timeout.** Robert reads slowly and carefully. He might spend 5 minutes reading a single parcel detail page. If the session times out and he's logged out, he has to remember his password (which is written on a Post-it note stuck to his iPad case). If there's a 2FA step, he's lost.

## What Would Make Him Abandon

- **Feeling embarrassed.** If the product makes him feel old, slow, or technologically incompetent, he'll close it and never tell anyone he tried. He won't complain — he'll just quietly stop using it.
- **No human support option.** If there's no phone number or easy way to reach a person, he won't struggle through it alone. He'll wait until Marcus visits.
- **Complicated data entry.** If adding his parcels requires information he can't easily find or forms that are confusing, he'll stop after one attempt.
- **Fear of breaking something.** If he's not confident that his actions are reversible (or that the app will ask him before doing something permanent), he'll stop touching things.
- **Jargon and complexity.** If the app feels like it's built for professional investors and tech people, he'll conclude it's not for him.

## What Would Make Him Stay

- A "My Properties" section that is the first thing he sees, front and center. A list. Not cards, not a map, not a dashboard — a simple list with the parcel name/location and the key numbers.
- Large, clear tap targets. Generous spacing. No tiny icons or three-dot menus.
- The ability to add a parcel by typing in the county and his name (owner lookup) rather than needing to know the APN format.
- Value estimates presented simply: "Based on recent sales of similar land nearby, this parcel is estimated to be worth approximately $15,000-18,000." Not a single precise number that invites argument.
- A "Print" button so he can print a summary of his properties and put it in his filing cabinet and give a copy to his attorney.
- Plain-English labels throughout: "Your Properties," "Estimated Value," "Annual Tax," "Size (acres)."
- Undo capabilities and confirmation dialogs in simple language: "Are you sure you want to remove this property from your list? You can add it back later."

## Signature Quote

"I just want to see my properties and know what they're worth."

## Testing Priority

Robert tests **accessibility, plain language, large-target usability, low-tech-comfort navigation, and the existing-owner (non-investor) use case.** He represents a real and underserved segment: long-term landowners who aren't investors, aren't tech-savvy, and just want simple portfolio visibility. If AcreOS can serve Robert, it can serve the millions of Americans who own rural land and have no idea what it's worth.
