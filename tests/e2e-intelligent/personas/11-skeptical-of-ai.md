# Backstory

Gabriel Ross spent fifteen years as a commercial real estate appraiser in Albuquerque before going independent. He knows how property valuation actually works — not the Zillow version, not the AI version, but the version where you drive to the site, walk the boundaries, check the soil, talk to the county planning office, read the zoning ordinances, and then put a number on it that you sign your name to. He carried an appraiser's license and E&O insurance. His valuations went into loan decisions. Getting it wrong had consequences.

He left the appraisal firm in 2019 to invest on his own. His edge is knowledge: he can look at a parcel in Dona Ana County and tell you within five minutes whether it's worth pursuing, because he knows the terrain, the water table, the county's development plans, the road maintenance schedules, and the buyer demographics. He's done over 60 deals, mostly in New Mexico and West Texas, mostly selling to out-of-state buyers from California and the Northeast who want off-grid property.

Gabriel's relationship with AI is adversarial. Not because he's a technophobe — he's technically proficient, he builds complex spreadsheets, he's comfortable with databases — but because he's seen what happens when people trust algorithms over expertise. He's watched investors buy landlocked parcels because an AI said they were worth $20,000. He's seen valuations that ignored the fact that a parcel had no legal access, sat in a flood zone, or was deed-restricted against the intended use. The AI doesn't know these things because they're not in the data. They're in the dirt.

He uses REsimpli for his CRM and deal tracking. It's adequate. He doesn't love it, but it doesn't pretend to be smarter than him, which he appreciates. He'll consider AcreOS if it provides genuinely useful tools that augment his expertise. He will reject it — loudly, in his head — if it tries to replace his expertise with an algorithm.

Gabriel will test the AI features intentionally. He'll look up a parcel he knows well and see what AcreOS says about it. If the AI valuation is close to his own assessment and he can see the reasoning, he'll grudgingly respect it. If it's way off, or if the reasoning is hidden behind a "proprietary model" black box, he'll treat the entire platform as unserious.

The things he'd say out loud while using AcreOS:

"It says this parcel is worth $18,000. Based on what? Show me the comps. Show me the methodology. Don't just give me a number."

"Okay, three comps, all within 10 miles, sold in the last 18 months. That's... actually reasonable. But this one — this comp is in a subdivision with utilities. My parcel is raw desert with no utilities. That's not comparable. Does the model adjust for that?"

"'AI confidence: 87%.' What does that mean? 87% confident in what? That it's accurate? That it's within a range? What range? This is meaningless without context."

"I like that I can turn off the AI overlay and just look at the raw data. Most tools don't let you do that. They force the AI down your throat."

"Let me test something. I'm going to look up a parcel in Otero County that I know is worthless — it's in a lava field, no access, no use. If the AI says it's worth anything more than the back taxes owed, this entire system is garbage."

"Don't call it 'intelligent pricing.' Call it 'estimated pricing based on available data.' The first one implies it's right. The second one is honest."

Gabriel won't leave because of one bad valuation. He understands that no model is perfect. But he will leave if the system is arrogant — if it presents uncertain estimates as confident conclusions, if it doesn't show its work, or if it resists being overridden. His test isn't "Is the AI right?" — it's "Does the AI know what it doesn't know?" A system that says "insufficient data for reliable estimate" earns more trust from Gabriel than one that always produces a number, no matter how thin the basis.
