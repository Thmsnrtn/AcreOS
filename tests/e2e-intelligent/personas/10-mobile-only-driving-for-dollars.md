# Backstory

Tasha Okonkwo is a dental hygienist in Savannah who spends her Saturdays doing something most of her coworkers find baffling: driving down unpaved roads in rural Georgia and South Carolina, looking at empty lots. She drives a white Honda CR-V with a phone mount on the dashboard, a cooler of water bottles on the back seat, and a Bluetooth speaker playing true crime podcasts while she scouts.

She got into real estate through house wholesaling. A friend from church introduced her to DealMachine — the app where you drive neighborhoods, identify distressed houses, and the app pulls owner info so you can send a letter. Tasha did that for eight months and closed two deals, making about $11,000 total. It worked, but the competition in Savannah was brutal. Every other wholesaler was driving the same neighborhoods, sending the same letters, making the same offers.

Her mentor — an older investor named Charles who she met at a Savannah REI meetup — suggested she try the same approach with vacant land. "Nobody's driving rural counties looking for land deals," he told her. "You'd have zero competition." He was right. On her first Saturday drive through Effingham County, she found six overgrown parcels that looked forgotten — tall grass, no posted signs, no tire tracks. She pulled the owner info through the county assessor (painfully slow on her phone) and sent letters to all six. One responded. She bought a 3-acre lot for $4,200 and sold it for $11,500 on Facebook Marketplace three weeks later.

That one deal rewired her brain. Now she drives every weekend. Her process is low-tech but effective: she drives, she spots, she pulls over, she takes photos, she drops a pin in Google Maps with a note. Then on Sunday evening, she sits at her kitchen table and looks up each parcel on the county website to get the APN, owner name, and mailing address. The Sunday research part takes 3-4 hours and it's the worst part. She wants an app that lets her do it in real time, from the driver's seat.

She knows about DealMachine's land features but found them thin — the app is clearly built for houses, and the parcel data for rural vacant land is spotty. She wants something purpose-built for land, with the same in-the-field usability she's used to from DealMachine.

Tasha uses her phone for everything. She doesn't own a laptop or desktop. Her phone is her office, her camera, her GPS, and her deal-tracking system. An app that requires a desktop for key features is immediately disqualified.

The things she'd say out loud while using AcreOS:

"Okay, I'm on County Road 119 and there's a lot on my left that looks completely abandoned. Let me pull up the map... where am I on this thing?"

"Can I just tap on the map where I am? I need to see the parcel boundaries. The county site shows them but it takes forever to load out here."

"Oh, it shows the owner's name and mailing address right here? And the last tax payment? That's better than DealMachine for land. Way better."

"Let me take a photo. How do I attach it to this parcel? I don't want to save it in my camera roll and then forget which lot it goes with."

"This is loading so slow. I only have two bars out here. Can it cache the map data when I'm on Wi-Fi before I leave the house?"

"I saved six parcels today. Can I see them all as a list? And can I export that list so I can send letters Monday?"

Tasha's evaluation happens in the field, not at a desk. If the app works on a rural Georgia road with two bars of LTE, in direct sunlight, operated with one thumb while she's deciding whether to pull over — that's the test. If it doesn't work in that context, the desktop experience is irrelevant. She'll try it on one Saturday drive. If she saves time compared to her Google Maps + county website process, she'll use it every week. If it's slower or clunkier, she'll go back to her pins.
