# Backstory

Robert Maple is the kind of man who keeps a leather-bound ledger on his desk even though everything is also in a spreadsheet. He's not a Luddite — he uses technology competently — but he believes in redundancy and he believes in records. His file cabinet has a hanging folder for every parcel he owns, each containing the deed, the title policy, the survey if he has one, tax receipts, and a single sheet of paper with his acquisition notes written in blue ink.

He owns 32 parcels spread across Florida, Georgia, and the Carolinas. Most are between 2 and 20 acres. Some he bought at tax deed auctions. Some he bought through direct mail campaigns years ago. A handful are seller-financed to buyers making monthly payments. He's not trying to get rich quick — he got over that in his forties. He wants steady, boring, appreciating assets that don't call him on Thanksgiving because the water heater broke.

Robert found land investing after twenty years in single-family rentals. He still owns four rental houses, managed by a property manager, but he stopped buying them in 2018. The liability, the maintenance, the tenants — it wore him down. Land was the antidote. No tenants, no toilets, no termites. He jokes about this at his local REIA meetup, where he's one of the older members and the only one who talks about land.

He heard about Pebble from another investor and used it briefly. He liked the concept but found it too focused on quick transactions. He doesn't need a CRM or a mail merge tool. He needs a portfolio dashboard — something that shows him what he owns, what it's worth now versus what he paid, what his tax obligations are this year, and whether any of his seller-finance notes are past due.

Robert approaches new software the way he approaches a new restaurant: he reads the menu carefully, orders something simple, and judges the establishment on whether that simple thing is done well. If the basic portfolio view is clean, accurate, and easy to read, he'll explore further. If it's cluttered with features he doesn't need or — worse — if the data feels unreliable, he'll leave a polite mental note that it's "not for me" and go back to his spreadsheet.

The things he'd say out loud while using AcreOS:

"Alright, let me add a couple of my parcels and see how this looks. I'll start with the ones in Marion County."

"Can I upload a PDF of the deed here? I want everything in one place, not scattered across Dropbox and email."

"This valuation says $45,000 but I know for a fact similar lots sold for $28,000 last year. Where is this number coming from?"

"I like this layout. It's clean. But I need to be able to print this page — my accountant doesn't use software, he uses paper."

"I don't need AI to tell me what to buy. I need it to track what I already own. Is there a mode for that?"

Robert won't rage-quit. He'll just quietly stop logging in. His patience is high, but his loyalty is to accuracy. If he enters data and it gets lost, if a value changes without a clear audit trail, if the app rearranges his view without asking — those are the things that erode his trust, slowly, until one day he realizes he hasn't opened it in three weeks and doesn't miss it.
