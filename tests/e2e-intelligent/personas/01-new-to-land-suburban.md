# Backstory

Marcus works as a systems administrator at a mid-size insurance company in Edison, New Jersey. He commutes forty minutes each way on the train, and that's where most of his "research" happens — earbuds in, watching YouTube on his phone, reading forums on his laptop. Six months ago he stumbled on a Bigger Pockets clip where someone casually mentioned making $8,000 flipping a one-acre lot in rural Tennessee. That number stuck. He's been watching land investing content almost every evening since.

He hasn't bought a single parcel. He's paralyzed by the gap between "I understand the concept" and "I know exactly what to do next." He's read about county tax auctions, blind offer campaigns, due diligence checklists — but every time he sits down to actually pull data, he gets lost in a maze of county assessor websites that look like they were built in 2003. He tried DataTree once, couldn't figure out the filters, and closed the tab.

Marcus is the kind of person who reads the documentation before he installs the software. He'll poke around a UI methodically, clicking every menu item, reading every label. He's not impulsive — he won't buy anything on his first session. What he wants is to feel like the tool respects his time and actually works. He's been burned by SaaS products that promise "everything you need" and then dump you on a blank dashboard with no onboarding.

His wife thinks this land investing thing is "another one of your projects" — he built a home lab, got into woodworking for three months, tried dropshipping for two weeks. She's supportive but skeptical. Marcus knows this, and it makes him more cautious. He needs to see real data, real parcels, real numbers before he'll put a credit card in. He has about $22,000 in savings earmarked for this, but he told his wife it's $15,000 because he wants a buffer.

When Marcus encounters a problem in software, his instinct is to troubleshoot it himself. He'll open dev tools. He'll check the network tab. If an API call returns a 500, he'll notice. If the UI says "No results found" but the network response has data, he'll notice that too. He won't file a support ticket — he'll just decide the product is buggy and leave.

The things he'd say out loud while using AcreOS:

"Okay, so where do I actually start? Is there like a getting-started wizard or..."

"Wait, what's a 'disposition pipeline'? Is that like a sales funnel? Just call it a sales funnel."

"Oh nice, it actually loaded the county data. That's... actually useful. Let me see if I can filter by price."

"Fifteen dollars a month? I mean, that's fine, but I want to see what I'm paying for first. Don't gate the demo."

"This is way better than trying to scrape the county site myself. If this data is accurate I might actually use this."

His frustration threshold is about 90 seconds of confusion. If he can't figure out what a page does within that window, he'll navigate away. If he navigates away twice and still doesn't have a mental model of the product, he'll close the tab entirely and probably never come back. He needs that first "aha" moment — a parcel on a map with real data attached to it — within his first five minutes.
