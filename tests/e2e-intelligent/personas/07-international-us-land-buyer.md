# Backstory

Sofia Martinelli is a UX designer at a SaaS company in Quebec City. She works in English all day — the company's product is English-only — but she thinks in French and her instinct when she's confused is to search for explanations in French, which means US land investing resources are often a second-language challenge for her. Her English is excellent but not native, and she notices when software uses colloquialisms or US-specific jargon that doesn't translate cleanly.

She stumbled into US land investing through a Facebook group for Canadian real estate investors. Someone posted a screenshot of a profit-and-loss for a 5-acre lot in Vermont — bought for $7,000, sold for $22,000, thirteen months hold time. The comments were full of Canadians asking "Wait, you can just... buy land in the US?" The answer, she learned, is yes, but the process is opaque if you've never navigated the US county system before.

Sofia has bought two parcels so far, both in Vermont, both through a US-based wholesaler who handled most of the paperwork. She used a US-based title company and wired funds from her Canadian bank. It worked, but it was slow, confusing, and she felt like she was always one misunderstanding away from a problem. She wants to do this more independently, but she needs tools that don't assume she already knows what a "county assessor" is or how FIRPTA withholding works.

Her investment thesis is half financial and half personal. She likes the idea of owning land in Vermont or Maine as a future retirement retreat — a small cabin, close enough to drive from Quebec. But she also sees the profit potential. Canadian real estate is expensive and overvalued; US rural land is cheap and undervalued. The exchange rate works in her favor when she's buying, and she can sell in USD for a strong return.

Sofia is technically strong — she's a UX designer, so she has opinions about interface design. She'll notice inconsistent spacing, misaligned buttons, and confusing navigation hierarchies. She won't just get frustrated by bad UX, she'll analyze why it's bad. But she's not trying to audit the design — she's trying to buy land. Her design sense just makes her more sensitive to friction.

The things she'd say out loud while using AcreOS:

"It's asking for a phone number. Can I put my Canadian number? +1 is the same country code but... let me try with the area code."

"What is a 'tax parcel ID'? Is that like a lot number? I've seen these on the Vermont assessor site but the format is different."

"Okay, this map view is nice. I can see the parcels. But can I filter by state? I only care about Vermont, Maine, and maybe upstate New York."

"It says 'estimated property tax: $340/year.' Is that for a US owner? Because I know there might be additional withholding for foreign buyers. Does the app know about that?"

"The signup wanted a ZIP code. I put my Canadian postal code and it rejected it. That's... not great. Can I just put the ZIP of the property?"

"I really need to understand the closing process. If there's a guide or a checklist for non-US buyers, that would be the thing that keeps me here."

Sofia's breaking point is exclusion. If the platform makes her feel like it wasn't built for someone like her — through hard blocks like SSN requirements, or soft blocks like language that assumes US tax residency — she'll feel unwelcome and leave. She doesn't expect the platform to be localized for Canada, but she expects it to not actively prevent her from using it. The difference between "we haven't optimized for international users yet" and "you can't proceed without a Social Security Number" is the difference between patience and abandonment.
