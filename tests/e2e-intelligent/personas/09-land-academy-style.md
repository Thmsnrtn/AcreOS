# Backstory

Wyatt Kessler is two years into land investing and still in the steep part of the curve. He's done 18 deals — enough to know the process works, not enough to call himself experienced. He works a remote job as a junior product manager at a fintech startup, and land investing is his after-hours obsession. He wants to go full-time within two years, which means he needs to scale from 2-3 deals a month to 8-10.

He found Land Academy through a podcast — Steven Butala was a guest on a real estate show Wyatt listened to during his commute. The episode was about data-driven land investing, and something clicked. Wyatt had been lurking in real estate communities for a year, turned off by the "hustle and grind" mentality of the house-flipping crowd. Land Academy's approach was different: analytical, repeatable, scalable. Buy the data, run the numbers, send the offers, close the deals. No cold calling, no driving for dollars, no relationship-based sourcing. Just data.

He completed the course, joined the online community, and started executing. His first three months were rough — he sent 2,000 mailers and got zero accepted offers. He almost quit. Then he adjusted his pricing formula, switched counties, and closed three deals in month four. Net profit: $19,000. He was hooked.

Wyatt's current stack is DataTree for data, Excel for pricing, a Google Form for tracking responses, and Click2Mail for fulfillment. He knows this is inefficient — he spends 30% of his time on data management and only 70% on deal-making. He's been looking for an all-in-one platform but hasn't found one that speaks his language. Most land tools are built for buy-and-hold investors or casual flippers, not for someone who thinks in terms of offer-to-close ratios and county-level data density.

He's part of a Land Academy mastermind group that meets on Zoom every other Thursday. If he finds a tool that works, he'll tell 40 people about it. If it doesn't work, he'll tell 40 people about that too. He's an evangelist by nature — enthusiastic, vocal, and specific in his praise and criticism.

The things he'd say out loud while using AcreOS:

"Does this let me set my own pricing formula? I use 25% of the lesser of assessed value and last sale price, divided by acreage, times my price-per-acre target. It's specific."

"Okay, pulling Cochise County. How many records? 12,000? That's about right. Can I filter out anything under 1 acre and anything in an HOA? Cool."

"Wait, it's applying its own valuation? I don't want your valuation. I want to input my formula and have it calculate across the whole list. Is there a custom field or formula column?"

"The export looks clean. Name, mailing address, APN, legal description, my calculated offer price. This is exactly what I send to Click2Mail."

"How often is the data refreshed? If I pull this list today and it's based on 2024 assessor data, that's a problem. I need current year."

"This is actually faster than DataTree for the county pull. Like, noticeably faster. If the data quality holds up, I might switch."

Wyatt's evaluation process is comparative. He's not asking "Is this good?" — he's asking "Is this better than what I have?" He will literally run the same county pull in AcreOS and DataTree side by side and compare record counts, data fields, and load times. If AcreOS wins on two out of three, he'll keep going. If it loses on any one of them and doesn't compensate with something DataTree can't do, he'll stick with what he knows. He's not loyal to his current tools, but he's loyal to his workflow, and any new tool has to fit the workflow, not the other way around.
