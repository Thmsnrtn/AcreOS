# Backstory

Eleanor Briggs retired three years ago from her job as an office manager at a dental practice in Sedona. She spent 28 years there — watching the practice grow from two hygienists to eight, managing the transition from paper charts to digital, training new front desk staff every time someone moved on. She was good at her job, organized and patient, but she was ready to stop.

Retirement has been... fine. She volunteers at the library on Tuesdays, takes a watercolor class, and walks the trails near her house most mornings. But she misses the feeling of having a project. Something with steps. Something where she can see progress. Her husband passed four years ago, and the house feels large and quiet in the evenings.

Her grandson Jaylen, who's 22 and studies finance at ASU, showed her a TikTok one Sunday during dinner. A young man was standing in front of an empty field explaining how he bought it for $2,000 and sold it for $8,000 by putting it on Facebook Marketplace. Eleanor was skeptical — "that sounds like one of those schemes" — but Jaylen walked her through the logic, and it made sense to her. Land is real. It doesn't go to zero. If you buy cheap enough, the downside is small.

She spent two weeks reading about it. Not watching videos — reading. She found blog posts, BiggerPockets articles, and a few Reddit threads. She understands the basic concept: find cheap land, buy it from a motivated seller, list it for more, wait for a buyer. She has about $8,000 she's comfortable using — not money she needs, just money she'd rather put to use than let sit in a savings account earning 0.4%.

Eleanor's relationship with technology is functional but fragile. She uses her iPhone for everything — texting, email, Facebook, Safari. She doesn't own a laptop anymore; she gave it to Jaylen when he started college. She can navigate apps that are well-designed, but she gets flustered by apps that assume she knows things. Dropdown menus that don't label what they're for. Filters that use abbreviations. Search bars that return nothing when she types a full sentence instead of a keyword.

She won't ask for help unless she's truly stuck. She'll tap around, read every word on the screen, try things. She's not in a hurry. But she has a low tolerance for feeling stupid. If an app makes her feel like she's supposed to already know something, she'll put the phone down and go do something else. Not angry — just deflated.

The things she'd say out loud while using AcreOS:

"Okay, I need to make the text bigger. Can I... oh, it's already a pretty good size. That's nice."

"What's an 'APN'? Is that like an address? I just want to search by state and price."

"Oh, there's a map. I like the map. Can I tap on these little dots? Oh, it shows the price. $3,200 for two acres in Texas. That seems reasonable."

"It wants me to create an account. Do I need to give my credit card? I'm not ready to pay for anything. I just want to look."

"Jaylen, can you come look at this? It says 'disposition pipeline' and I have no idea what that means. Is that where I sell it?"

"I found one I like. It's $4,500 in New Mexico. How do I... do I just click 'buy'? Surely it's not that simple. Is there a next-steps section?"

Eleanor won't abandon AcreOS in a dramatic moment. She'll set her phone down, say "I'll come back to this later," and then probably won't. The app has to earn her return by making her feel capable during this first session. If she finds one parcel, understands what it is, and feels like she could figure out the next step — even if she doesn't take it today — she'll come back. If she feels lost, she'll mention to Jaylen that "that land app was a little confusing" and that'll be the end of it.
