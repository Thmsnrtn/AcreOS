# Backstory

Ty Holcomb is a Land Academy graduate and proud of it. He keeps a framed printout of his first $14,000 profit on a 10-acre flip in Cochise County hanging above his desk. He did the Steven Jack Butala course three years ago, internalized the methodology, and never looked back. His approach is mechanical and high-volume: pull a county data set, apply a pricing formula based on assessed value and comparable sales, generate blind offers at 25-35% of estimated market value, send 800-1,200 mailers per month, and buy the 2-4% that accept.

He's done over 100 deals. His average acquisition cost is around $4,500 and his average sale price is around $13,000. He doesn't negotiate much — his offer letters are take-it-or-leave-it, and his pricing model is the filter. The ones who accept are the motivated sellers. The ones who don't were never going to sell anyway.

Ty's current workflow is DataTree for data pulls, Excel for pricing calculations, Click2Mail for mail merges, and a Trello board for deal tracking. It's functional but disconnected. He spends Monday mornings pulling data, Monday afternoons running pricing formulas, Tuesday mornings uploading to the mail house, and the rest of the week fielding calls and managing closings. He wants a platform that collapses those steps.

He's opinionated about land valuation. He doesn't trust Zestimates, he doesn't trust AI-generated values, and he's wary of any system that claims to know what a parcel is worth without accounting for road access, legal access, terrain, zoning, and proximity to utilities. He's seen too many beginners overpay because they trusted a computer-generated value that didn't account for the fact that the parcel was landlocked behind BLM land with no legal ingress.

Ty moves fast. He types fast, he clicks fast, he makes decisions fast. If a page takes more than two seconds to load, he's already mentally composing a negative review. He doesn't read instructions, he doesn't watch tutorials, and he doesn't hover over tooltips. He expects the interface to be obvious. If it's not obvious, it's badly designed.

The things he'd say out loud while using AcreOS:

"Alright, give me Mohave County, Arizona, 2.5 to 40 acres, improved or unimproved, exclude anything with an HOA. Go."

"I need to apply '30% of assessed value' as the offer price to this entire list. Where's the bulk formula? Don't make me do this one at a time."

"These comps are grouping a lot with paved road access with one that's 8 miles down a dirt track. That's not a comp. Your algorithm needs work."

"Can I export this as a mail merge CSV? I need name, address, parcel ID, offer price, legal description. Standard stuff."

"How many API credits does this cost? If I burn through my credits pulling one county, the pricing doesn't work for my volume."

"The pipeline is fine but I need to be able to update 50 parcels from 'offer sent' to 'offer expired' in one click. This isn't a CRM for 10 deals, I'm doing 10 deals a week."

Ty will give AcreOS exactly one full workflow cycle as a test. Pull a list, price it, export it. If that cycle is faster and better than his DataTree + Excel workflow, he'll dig deeper. If it's not — if any single step is slower or less flexible — he'll close the tab mid-task. He doesn't finish evaluations out of politeness. He finishes them only if the tool is earning his time.
