# Seller Financing for Land: The Complete Operational Guide

Seller financing is the most powerful tool in a land investor's arsenal. It turns a one-time flip profit into recurring monthly income, lets you sell at a higher price point, and creates a passive revenue stream that compounds over time.

But most investors treat it casually — handshake terms, spreadsheet tracking, and no awareness of the federal regulations that apply. That's a liability waiting to happen.

This guide covers the operational reality of seller financing: how to structure notes, manage payments, stay compliant, and build a note portfolio that actually generates the passive income everyone talks about.

## Why Seller Finance?

Three reasons:

**1. Higher sale price.** Buyers who can't qualify for traditional financing will pay a premium for terms. A parcel you'd sell for $12,000 cash can sell for $15,000-18,000 with seller financing. The buyer pays more because they're paying over time; you earn more because you're providing a service (credit).

**2. Recurring revenue.** Instead of a $12,000 lump sum, you get $298/month for 48 months. That's $14,304 total — $2,304 more than the cash price — and it comes in every month whether you're working or not.

**3. Portfolio compounding.** Five notes at $298/month is $1,490/month in passive income. Ten notes is $2,980. Twenty notes starts replacing a salary. Each deal you seller-finance adds another stream. The Freedom Meter in AcreOS tracks exactly this progression.

## How to Structure a Note

Every seller-financed deal needs these terms defined:

**Purchase price:** The total amount the buyer is paying for the property. This is typically higher than what you'd accept for a cash sale.

**Down payment:** Usually 10-30% of the purchase price. The down payment covers your acquisition cost (or most of it), which means you're cash-flow positive from day one. A larger down payment also reduces your risk — a buyer with $3,000 invested is less likely to default than one with $500 invested.

**Interest rate:** The annual percentage rate on the remaining balance. For land, rates typically range from 7-12% depending on the market and the buyer's profile. Keep this below your state's usury limit (more on this in the compliance section).

**Term length:** How many months the buyer has to pay off the balance. Common terms are 24, 36, 48, or 60 months. Longer terms mean lower monthly payments (easier for the buyer) but more interest income for you.

**Monthly payment:** Calculated from the remaining balance (purchase price minus down payment), the interest rate, and the term length using standard amortization.

**Balloon payment:** Some notes include a balloon payment — a large lump sum due at the end of the term. This keeps monthly payments lower while ensuring you get the balance paid off. Balloon payments have regulatory implications under Dodd-Frank (see below).

### Example: The Math

- **Purchase price:** $15,000
- **Down payment:** $3,000 (20%)
- **Financed amount:** $12,000
- **Interest rate:** 9% annually
- **Term:** 48 months

**Monthly payment:** $298.26

Over 48 months, the buyer pays $3,000 down + $14,316.48 in payments = $17,316.48 total.

Your profit: $17,316 - $15,000 = $2,316 in interest income, plus whatever margin you had between your acquisition cost and the $15,000 sale price.

If you acquired the parcel for $4,000, your total return is $13,316 on a $4,000 investment — a 333% ROI over 4 years.

## Payment Management

This is where most investors drop the ball. Collecting payments consistently requires systems, not memory.

**Payment methods:** Accept ACH (bank transfer), credit/debit card, or check. ACH is preferred — lowest fees, most reliable, can be automated with recurring payments. Set this up through Stripe Connect or a similar payment processor.

**Payment schedule:** Monthly, due on the same date every month (typically the 1st or 15th). Consistency makes bookkeeping easier and sets clear expectations with the buyer.

**Grace period:** Standard is 10-15 days. If payment is due on the 1st, it's not late until the 16th. This accounts for mail delays, bank processing, and life happening.

**Late fees:** Typically 5% of the monthly payment or a flat $25-50, whichever is greater. Late fees should be defined in the promissory note and disclosed to the buyer at signing.

**What to do when a payment is late:**

1. **Day 1-10 (grace period):** No action needed.
2. **Day 11:** Send a friendly reminder. "Your payment of $298 was due on the 1st. Please submit at your earliest convenience."
3. **Day 16:** Late fee applied. Send a notice: "A late fee of $15 has been applied to your account."
4. **Day 21:** Phone call or direct message. "I noticed your payment hasn't come through. Is everything okay? Let's figure this out."
5. **Day 30:** Formal notice of default. "Per the terms of your promissory note, your account is 30 days past due. Please contact me within 10 days to arrange payment."
6. **Day 60+:** Consult your contract terms for acceleration and remedies. This is where having a proper promissory note (reviewed by an attorney) matters.

Most late payments are resolved at step 2 or 3. Having an automated system that sends these reminders on schedule means you don't have to remember to follow up — and you don't have to be the bad guy.

## Dodd-Frank Compliance

This is the section most investors skip, and it's the one that can cost you the most.

The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act (2010) includes provisions that apply to seller-financed transactions. The relevant rules are in the Truth in Lending Act (TILA) and the SAFE Act, as amended by Dodd-Frank.

### The 3 Key Rules

**1. Interest rate limits (usury laws):** Every state has a maximum interest rate for seller-financed transactions. These range from 8% (some states) to 18% or no limit (other states). If your note's interest rate exceeds the state limit, the note may be unenforceable, and you may face penalties. Always check the usury limit for the state where the property is located.

**2. Balloon payment restrictions:** Under Dodd-Frank, if you're not exempt (see below), balloon payments are generally prohibited for seller-financed transactions with consumers. If your note has a balloon, you need to verify you qualify for an exemption or structure the note without one.

**3. Ability-to-repay (ATR):** If you originate 3+ seller-financed transactions in a 12-month period, you may be classified as a "loan originator" and subject to the ATR rule. This means you must make a reasonable, good-faith determination that the borrower can repay the loan based on their income and debts. For land notes (where the property is not the borrower's primary residence), many transactions are exempt — but the line is blurry, and compliance is the safe play.

### Which Transactions Are Exempt?

The most common exemption for land investors: if the property is **not** the buyer's primary dwelling and the buyer does not intend to build a primary dwelling on it, most Dodd-Frank provisions don't apply. Vacant land sold for investment, recreation, or agricultural purposes is generally exempt.

However: if the buyer intends to build a home on the land and it will be their primary residence, full Dodd-Frank compliance applies. This is a disclosure and documentation requirement — not a reason to avoid the deal, but a reason to have your paperwork in order.

### The Penalties

Violations of Dodd-Frank lending rules can result in:
- The borrower can rescind the transaction (you have to unwind the deal)
- Statutory damages up to $4,000 per violation
- Attorney's fees and court costs if the borrower sues
- Potential regulatory action if you're found to be operating as an unlicensed loan originator

The cost of compliance is low. The cost of a violation is high. Always err on the side of caution.

## The Borrower Relationship

Your note holders are long-term customers. Treat them well:

**Communication:** Respond to inquiries within 24 hours. If they ask for their payoff balance, provide it the same day. If they're going through a rough patch, talk about it — a payment plan modification is better than a default.

**Annual statements:** Send each borrower an annual statement showing: total payments made in the calendar year, principal paid, interest paid, remaining balance. They need this for their records, and it's good practice.

**Payoff quotes:** When a borrower wants to pay off their note early, provide a payoff quote promptly. Include the remaining principal, any accrued interest, and the payoff-good-through date. Don't penalize early payoff unless it's explicitly in your note (and consider not including prepayment penalties — they create friction and ill will).

**Record keeping:** Maintain records of every payment, every communication, every statement, and every document for the life of the note plus 3 years. If there's ever a dispute, your records are your defense.

## Building a Note Portfolio

The goal isn't one note — it's a portfolio. Here's how to think about it:

**Diversify by county and state.** Don't put all your notes in one market. If that market declines, your entire passive income stream is at risk.

**Diversify by term length.** Mix of shorter (24-month) and longer (60-month) notes gives you both quick returns and steady long-term income.

**Monitor consistently.** Review your note portfolio monthly: payment status, delinquency rate, average days late. Catch problems early before they become defaults.

**Reinvest.** As notes pay off, use the capital to acquire new parcels and create new notes. The compounding effect is real — 5 active notes becomes 10 becomes 20, and your monthly passive income grows with each cycle.
